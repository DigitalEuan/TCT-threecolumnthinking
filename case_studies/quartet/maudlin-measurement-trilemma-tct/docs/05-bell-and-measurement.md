# 05 — Bell, "Against 'Measurement'" (1990), and the trilemma

## The complaint

In *Physics World* (August 1990), John Bell pressed a complaint he had been
making for decades: the vocabulary of quantum foundations — 'measurement',
'observer', 'apparatus', 'macroscopic' — has no place in an exact fundamental
theory. These words are too vague to say where the dividing line sits between
the quantum system and the classical instrument. The textbook formulation
shunts the vagueness into a "cut", and then does not say where the cut is.

Famous targets of the essay: the word 'measurement' itself as a primitive;
the vagueness of 'apparatus'; the inconsistency of treating some systems with
Schrödinger evolution and others with collapse, without a criterion for which
is which. (Thirty years on, Physics World revisited the essay — the
uncertainty over terms such as 'apparatus' had not evaporated.)

## Why the trilemma is the precise form of the complaint

Bell's essay is a diagnosis of vagueness; the trilemma is a differential
diagnosis. Instead of "measurement is vague", it says:

1. **C1** (completeness of ψ), **C2** (always-linear evolution), **C3**
   (single outcomes) — three exact claims;
2. a single concrete experiment (spin + detector) shows the three cannot hold
   together;
3. therefore any exact theory must say which of the three it drops.

This converts Bell's methodological complaint into a design specification for
interpretations. A theory that does not say which claim it sacrifices is not
"mysterious" — it is *incomplete*. Bell's own preferred direction is visible
in the essay's sympathies: take the ontology seriously (the Bohmian door:
distinguish ψ from the physical state it describes), and never let
'measurement' do fundamental work.

## Bell's own ordering of worries

Bell distinguished, in effect, several problems that the phrase "the
measurement problem" blurs — the problem of outcomes (why one result), the
problem of the classical/quantum cut, and the problem of the vocabulary of
observation. Maudlin's 1995 essay likewise insists on *separating* distinct
measurement problems before solving any of them. The trilemma reading used in
this study (docs/01) is the standard reconstruction of that sharpening
(cited in the literature as "Maudlin's trilemma"; see docs/08 and the
premise note below).

## Premise note (TCT discipline)

The study's framing — "Maudlin (1995) gave the problem the form of three
incompatible claims" — is carried as a *conversational premise* (axiom-class),
per TCT §3: it is a claim about a text, not a derivation. The derivations in
this repository (scripts 01–05, Lean tier) establish the internal structure:
given the three claims and the standard linear model of measurement
interaction, the incompatibility and the three-doors map follow exactly.
